Zone 2 cardio is low-intensity aerobic training — done at a pace easy enough to hold a full conversation — that builds your cardiovascular base, improves fat metabolism, and supports recovery without competing with your lifting sessions. It's the opposite of going hard: the entire point is staying comfortable enough to sustain it for a long time, repeatedly, week after week.

## What it actually feels like

Zone 2 should never feel like a struggle. You're not pushing yourself hard, gasping for air, or watching the clock to see when it ends. It should feel like a comfortable, sustainable effort — the kind of pace you could genuinely hold for 30, 45, or 60 minutes without it draining you for the rest of the day.

This is the part most people get wrong when they first try it. It feels almost too easy, and that instinct to push harder is exactly what undermines the training effect. Zone 2 isn't supposed to be hard. If you finish a session feeling wrecked, you weren't doing Zone 2 — you were doing Zone 3 or 4, which is a different kind of training with a different purpose.

## What is Zone 2, technically?

Zone 2 refers to a heart rate range — roughly 60–70% of your maximum heart rate — where your body relies primarily on fat as a fuel source rather than carbohydrate. A simple way to estimate your max heart rate is 220 minus your age, though the [Tanaka formula](/calculators/heart-rate-zones) (208 − 0.7 × age) is considered more accurate across age groups. Multiply your max heart rate by 0.6 and 0.7 to find your personal Zone 2 range, or use the [heart rate zones calculator](/calculators/heart-rate-zones) to get your exact numbers along with all five training zones.

## The benefits of Zone 2 training

**Improved fat metabolism.** At this intensity, your body shifts toward burning fat as its primary fuel source rather than glycogen. Training consistently in this zone improves your body's efficiency at using fat for energy, which has knock-on benefits for endurance and metabolic health more broadly.

**Better heart and lung function.** Zone 2 training is one of the most effective ways to build aerobic capacity over time — your heart's stroke volume improves (it pumps more blood per beat), your resting heart rate tends to drop, and your lungs become more efficient at extracting oxygen. These adaptations build gradually over months of consistent training.

**Reduced injury risk.** Lower-intensity training places far less strain on your muscles, tendons, ligaments, and joints compared to high-intensity work. This matters because it means you can train this way far more frequently — daily, in many cases — without accumulating the kind of fatigue and impact stress that leads to overuse injuries.

**Endurance gains.** Mitochondria are the structures inside your cells responsible for producing energy aerobically, and Zone 2 training is uniquely effective at stimulating their growth. More and healthier mitochondria mean a better-functioning aerobic engine — which improves your capacity for sustained effort across almost every kind of physical activity, not just cardio.

## Why Zone 2 fits well alongside lifting

If you're training for strength or muscle, the appeal of Zone 2 is that it doesn't compete with that goal the way high-intensity cardio can. A hard interval session the day after heavy squats adds real fatigue on top of fatigue. A Zone 2 session at an easy, comfortable pace generally doesn't — which is why it's a common recommendation for hybrid athletes trying to build endurance without undermining their lifting recovery.

## How much Zone 2 should you do?

For beginners starting alongside a lifting programme:

- **Weeks 1–4:** 2 sessions per week, 20–30 minutes each
- **Weeks 5–8:** 2–3 sessions, 30–40 minutes each
- **Maintenance:** 3 sessions, 45 minutes each

A reasonable target is 90–150 minutes of Zone 2 per week for meaningful cardiovascular adaptation over time.

## Best Zone 2 activities for lifters

1. **Cycling (indoor or outdoor)** — low impact, easy to control intensity precisely
2. **Brisk walking or incline treadmill** — the easiest way to stay locked into the zone, with essentially zero injury risk
3. **Rowing machine** — full body, useful on days you want to spare your legs
4. **Swimming** — excellent if joints need a break from impact entirely

Running is harder to recommend as your only Zone 2 tool when starting out, simply because the impact and effort required to stay at a genuinely easy pace can be inconsistent for newer runners — it's easy to drift out of the zone without realising it.

## How to know you're actually in Zone 2

The talk test remains the simplest practical check: you should be able to speak in full sentences while feeling slightly breathless. If you can comfortably sing, you're going too easy. If you can't speak at all, you've drifted out of Zone 2.

A chest strap heart rate monitor tends to be more accurate than wrist-based optical sensors for this kind of steady, sustained effort.

## Common mistakes

- **Drifting into Zone 3** — going hard enough to be tiring but not hard enough to count as proper high-intensity work. This is sometimes called "junk miles" because it produces fatigue without the benefits of either true Zone 2 or genuine HIIT.
- **Pairing it with leg day** — doing a hard Zone 2 session immediately after heavy lower body training defeats the recovery-friendly purpose of the whole approach.
- **Skipping it because it feels too easy** — that feeling is the entire point. The adaptation comes from the cumulative volume of easy effort over weeks and months, not from any single session feeling like an achievement.
