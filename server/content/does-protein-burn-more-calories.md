## The claim you've probably heard

Somewhere in your fitness journey, someone has told you that protein "burns more calories" or even that it's a "negative calorie" food — that you burn more digesting it than it actually contains. It gets repeated constantly in fitness content, usually as a reason to eat enormous amounts of protein.

Like most fitness claims, there's a kernel of truth buried under a lot of exaggeration. Protein genuinely does cost your body more energy to process than carbohydrates or fat. But the size of that effect — and what it actually means for your physique — is very different from how it's usually presented.

Let's clear it up properly.

---

## What is the thermic effect of food?

Every time you eat, your body spends energy digesting, absorbing, transporting, and storing that food. This process is called the thermic effect of food, often abbreviated to TEF. It's one of the three main components of your total daily energy expenditure, alongside your basal metabolic rate (the energy you burn at rest) and the energy you burn through physical activity.

For most people, TEF accounts for around 10% of total daily calorie expenditure. If you burn 2,500 calories a day, roughly 250 of those are spent simply processing the food you eat.

Here's where it gets interesting: that 10% is an average across a mixed diet. The actual thermic effect varies enormously depending on what you eat — because the three macronutrients are not processed equally.

---

## Protein costs the most to digest

This is the heart of the matter. Each macronutrient has its own thermic effect, expressed as the percentage of its own calories burned during processing:

| Macronutrient | Thermic effect (% of its calories burned digesting it) |
|---------------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| Protein | 20–30% |
| Carbohydrates | 5–10% |
| Fat | 0–3% |

Protein is in a completely different league. When you eat 100 calories of protein, your body burns roughly 20 to 30 of those calories just to process it — leaving you with a net of 70 to 80 calories actually absorbed and available to your body.

Compare that to fat. Eat 100 calories of fat and your body burns almost nothing processing it — you net close to the full 100 calories.

The reason protein is so metabolically expensive comes down to chemistry. Protein is made of amino acids, and breaking dietary protein down into those amino acids, then reassembling them into the proteins your body needs, is a complex, energy-hungry process. Carbohydrates and fats are far simpler for the body to break down and store.

---

## So does protein "burn more calories"? Yes — but not how you think

Here's the important distinction. Protein doesn't burn more calories in some magical, fat-melting sense. What actually happens is that **a significant chunk of protein's own calories are spent during its digestion**, so the net energy you absorb from protein is lower than the number on the label suggests.

It's less "protein burns fat" and more "protein partially burns itself."

This is a real, measurable effect. It's not a myth. But the magnitude is where the exaggeration creeps in.

---

## How big is the effect, really?

Let's run actual numbers. Imagine you swap 200 calories of carbohydrates for 200 calories of protein in your daily diet:

- **200 calories of carbs** → about 15 calories burned digesting → **185 calories netted**
- **200 calories of protein** → about 50 calories burned digesting → **150 calories netted**

The difference is around **35 calories per day**.

Over a month, that adds up to roughly 1,000 calories — about the equivalent of 0.13kg of fat. Real, but modest. It is not going to transform your body composition on its own. Anyone telling you that you can eat in a surplus and stay lean purely because of protein's thermic effect is selling you a fantasy.

If you dramatically increased your protein — say, replacing 600 calories of fat with 600 calories of protein daily — the difference grows to around 100 calories per day. More meaningful, but still in the range of a small daily walk, not a metabolic miracle.

---

## Why protein still matters enormously

Here's the irony: the thermic effect is probably the *least* important reason to prioritise protein. The real benefits are far more powerful.

**Satiety.** Protein is by far the most filling of the three macronutrients. Meals high in protein keep you fuller for longer, which naturally reduces your overall calorie intake without conscious effort. This appetite-suppressing effect almost certainly does more for fat loss than the thermic effect ever could.

**Muscle preservation in a deficit.** When you're eating in a calorie deficit to lose fat, your body can break down muscle tissue for energy alongside fat. Adequate protein intake signals your body to preserve muscle, ensuring that the weight you lose is overwhelmingly fat rather than hard-earned muscle. This is critical — losing muscle slows your metabolism and leaves you looking "skinny fat" rather than lean.

**Muscle building.** Protein provides the amino acids your body uses to repair and build muscle tissue after training. More muscle means a higher resting metabolic rate, meaning you burn more calories around the clock — a far bigger long-term metabolic benefit than the thermic effect of any single meal.

---

## How much protein do you actually need?

This is where the "more protein burns more calories" logic falls apart as a reason to eat enormous amounts. The research is clear that muscle protein synthesis is maximised at around 0.7 to 0.8 grams of protein per pound of bodyweight per day. For an 80kg (176lb) person, that's about 140 grams daily.

Beyond that threshold, additional protein doesn't build more muscle. And while it does carry a higher thermic effect than carbs or fat, the modest calorie cost doesn't justify crowding out the carbohydrates that fuel your training or the fats that support your hormones.

Eating 300 grams of protein a day "to burn more calories" is a poor strategy. You'd net a handful of extra calories burned while sacrificing the training fuel and hormonal support that actually drive results. ## The honest bottom line

Does eating protein burn more calories? Yes. Protein has a thermic effect two to three times higher than carbohydrates and many times higher than fat, meaning a meaningful portion of protein's own calories are spent during digestion. This is genuine, measurable, and worth understanding.

But the effect is modest — tens of calories per day, not hundreds. It is a helpful tailwind for fat loss, not the engine.

The real reasons to prioritise protein are its unmatched ability to keep you full, its role in preserving muscle during a diet, and its contribution to building the muscle that raises your metabolism long-term. Hit your protein target of 0.7 to 0.8 grams per pound of bodyweight, and let the thermic effect be a small bonus on top of those far more important benefits.

Eat enough protein. Don't overdo it chasing a metabolic effect that barely moves the needle. And spend the rest of your calories fuelling the training that actually changes your body.
