## The most researched supplement in sports science

If you've spent any time in a gym or around fitness content, you've heard about creatine. Unlike most supplements, which are built on marketing copy and weak studies, creatine has decades of research behind it across hundreds of trials and thousands of participants. It is not a grey area. The science is about as settled as sports nutrition science gets.

So let's go through it properly — what creatine is, what it does, whether it actually works, how to take it, and what the side effects are.

---

## What is creatine?

Creatine is a naturally occurring compound made from three amino acids — arginine, glycine, and methionine. Your liver, kidneys, and pancreas produce roughly 1–2 grams of creatine per day. You also get creatine from food, primarily from red meat and fish — a 200g steak contains approximately 1 gram of creatine.

About 95% of your body's creatine is stored in skeletal muscle as phosphocreatine (PCr). The other 5% is found in your brain, liver, and kidneys.

Phosphocreatine's primary job is to rapidly regenerate ATP — adenosine triphosphate — the molecule your muscles use for energy. When you perform a short, high-intensity effort (a heavy deadlift, a sprint, an explosive jump), your muscles burn through ATP extremely quickly. Phosphocreatine donates a phosphate group to replenish ATP almost instantly, extending how long and how hard you can work before fatigue sets in.

---

## Does creatine actually work?

Yes. This is about as close to a consensus as sports nutrition science gets.

A 2003 meta-analysis examining 22 studies found that creatine supplementation increased maximal strength by an average of 8% and maximal work capacity by 14% compared to placebo. More recent analyses consistently confirm these findings.

What creatine specifically improves:

**Maximal strength.** Bench press, squat, deadlift one-rep max performance consistently improves with creatine supplementation. The effect is modest but real and well-documented.

**High-intensity performance.** Sprint speed, jump height, repeated sprint ability, and explosive athletic performance all show improvements. The effect is most pronounced in activities lasting under 30 seconds.

**Muscle mass.** Creatine does not build muscle directly. What it does is allow you to train harder over time — more weight, more reps, better quality sessions — which leads to greater muscle growth compared to training without it. In most studies comparing creatine to placebo in a resistance training program, the creatine group builds more muscle over the study period. There is also an initial water retention effect where muscles pull in more water (discussed below).

**Training volume.** When your PCr stores are saturated, you can sustain high-intensity output for longer before fatigue. This means more reps at a given weight, which drives more progressive overload over weeks and months.

## Who does and doesn't respond to creatine

Approximately 25–30% of people are "non-responders" — their performance does not meaningfully improve with creatine supplementation. This is largely because their muscles are already near-saturated with creatine from dietary intake. People who eat large amounts of red meat and fish regularly tend to have higher baseline creatine levels and see less benefit from supplementation.

Vegetarians and vegans, who have no dietary creatine intake, typically see the most pronounced response — both in performance and muscle mass outcomes.

The honest message: creatine works for most people, but not everyone. The only way to know if you're a responder is to try it.

## How to take creatine

**What form:** Creatine monohydrate. Not creatine ethyl ester, not buffered creatine, not creatine HCl. Monohydrate is the form used in virtually all the research, it is the cheapest, and there is no credible evidence that the more expensive forms are more effective. Ignore the marketing.

**How much:** 3–5 grams per day. This is the evidence-based daily dose for maintenance. The timing, the liquid you mix it with, and whether you take it before or after training do not meaningfully affect the outcome. Consistency matters far more than timing.

**Loading phase — necessary or not?** Some protocols recommend a loading phase of 20g per day (split into four 5g doses) for 5–7 days to saturate muscles faster, followed by a 3–5g maintenance dose. Loading does accelerate saturation — you'll reach the same level in one week that would otherwise take 3–4 weeks. But you reach the same total saturation either way. If you're not in a hurry, just take 3–5g daily and you'll be fully saturated within a month.

**When to take it:** Timing is largely irrelevant. Some evidence suggests that taking creatine close to your training session (before or after) may have a slight edge over taking it at a random time, but the difference is small. What matters is that you take it every day. Taking it with a meal slightly improves absorption.

**Cycling — necessary?** No. There is no credible evidence that cycling creatine (taking breaks) provides any benefit. Your body's natural creatine production does downregulate slightly during supplementation, but this reverses within weeks of stopping and has no known negative long-term consequences. Most people simply take it indefinitely.

## Side effects — what's real and what's myth

**Weight gain — real, but not fat.** Most people gain 1–2kg in the first week or two of creatine supplementation. This is water. Creatine increases the osmotic draw on muscle cells, meaning they pull in more water. This is not fat gain. The water weight is intramuscular — your muscles look and feel slightly fuller, not puffy or bloated. Some people find this beneficial (muscles appear larger); some find the scale movement confusing.

**Kidney damage — a myth.** This is the most persistent creatine myth and it has been thoroughly debunked. The concern originated from a misunderstanding: creatinine (a creatine breakdown product) is a kidney function marker, and creatine supplementation raises creatinine slightly. Early researchers worried this indicated kidney stress. Subsequent research in healthy individuals has found no evidence of kidney damage at normal doses. Studies following athletes for years on creatine supplementation show no impairment of kidney function. The caveat: if you have pre-existing kidney disease, consult a doctor before supplementing.

**Dehydration and cramping — no consistent evidence.** Multiple large studies in athletes have found no increase in cramping, dehydration, or muscle pulls with creatine supplementation. Some earlier case reports suggested a link but controlled studies have not confirmed it.

**Hair loss — some evidence, but limited.** A 2009 study in rugby players found that creatine supplementation raised levels of DHT (dihydrotestosterone), a hormone linked to male pattern baldness, by approximately 56% over three weeks. This is the most credible concern from the research. However: this was a single study, DHT levels normalised after the loading phase, and no study has yet demonstrated an actual increase in hair loss from creatine supplementation. If you have a strong family history of male pattern baldness and are concerned, the evidence is not strong enough to say creatine definitely accelerates it — but it is not zero.

**Gastrointestinal discomfort — real for some people.** Some people experience stomach discomfort, particularly during a loading phase. This is minimised by splitting doses, taking creatine with food, and staying well hydrated. If you're sensitive, skip the loading phase entirely and just take 3–5g daily with a meal.

## Creatine and the brain

Research into creatine's cognitive effects is genuinely interesting and growing. Since the brain uses phosphocreatine for energy just as muscles do, supplementation appears to improve cognitive performance during periods of stress, sleep deprivation, and mental fatigue. Studies have found improvements in working memory and processing speed in sleep-deprived participants. Vegetarians and vegans — who again have no dietary creatine intake — show the most pronounced cognitive improvements.

This is still an active research area, but the early evidence is promising enough that creatine's benefits may extend well beyond athletic performance.

---

## The bottom line

Creatine monohydrate is the most rigorously studied supplement in sports science. The evidence for its effectiveness in improving strength, power output, and muscle gain over time is extensive and consistent. It is safe for healthy individuals, cheap, and straightforward to use.

Take 3–5 grams of creatine monohydrate daily. Mix it with water, juice, or add it to your protein shake. Take it consistently. Give it 4–6 weeks before evaluating whether it is working for you.

There is no other supplement with this level of evidence behind it. If you are training seriously and not taking creatine, you are leaving a well-documented, low-risk performance improvement on the table.
