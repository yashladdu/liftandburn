The treadmill itself is not bad for your knees. Running on a treadmill is actually slightly easier on your joints than running outdoors — the belt surface absorbs more impact than concrete or asphalt. What damages knees is not the treadmill; it is running with poor mechanics, running too much too soon, or running when the body is not conditioned for it.

That said, knee pain on the treadmill is common enough that it is worth understanding exactly what causes it and how to avoid it.

---

## Treadmill vs outdoor running — which is harder on your knees?

The treadmill surface is softer and more forgiving than pavement. Most modern treadmills have cushioned decks that reduce ground reaction forces by around 10–30% compared to running on asphalt. From a pure impact standpoint, running indoors on a treadmill is gentler on the knees than running outside on hard surfaces.

There is one meaningful difference that can work against you: the treadmill belt assists the backward leg drive slightly, which changes gait mechanics for some runners. Stride length tends to be slightly longer on a treadmill, which can increase the load on the knee joint at heel strike if it pushes your footstrike too far in front of your centre of mass. This is a technique issue that becomes relevant when someone transfers training between outdoor and treadmill running — not a reason to avoid treadmills.

The net verdict: treadmill running is not harder on knees than outdoor running. For most people it is modestly easier.

## What actually causes knee pain on a treadmill

**Running volume increases too quickly.** The most common cause of treadmill-related knee pain — and running-related knee pain in general — is doing too much too soon. The cardiovascular system adapts faster than tendons, ligaments, and cartilage. You can feel fit enough to run further and faster long before your joints are conditioned to handle the load. Adding more than 10% to your weekly running volume creates a meaningful injury risk regardless of where you run.

**Poor running form.** Overstriding — landing with the foot well in front of the knee and hips — dramatically increases the braking force through the knee joint with each step. A shorter, faster stride with a footstrike closer to directly under the body reduces knee stress significantly. Cadence (steps per minute) around 170–180 is often cited as the target range; on a treadmill you can experiment with this more easily than outdoors.

**Weak glutes and hips.** Knee pain during running is often not a knee problem. Weak hip abductors and glutes allow the knee to track inward on each footstrike — a movement called knee valgus — which increases the stress on the medial knee structures. Strengthening the glutes, hip abductors, and hamstrings reduces this loading pattern significantly. Exercises like Romanian deadlifts, hip thrusts, and lateral band walks address the root cause rather than the symptom.

**Running downhill or at steep negative incline.** Descending — whether outdoors or on a treadmill set to a negative incline — dramatically increases the eccentric load on the quadriceps and the compressive forces on the kneecap (patella). Flat or slight positive incline running is considerably more knee-friendly than downhill.

**Pre-existing conditions.** Runner's knee (patellofemoral pain syndrome), IT band syndrome, and patellar tendinopathy are all common in runners and can be aggravated by treadmill use regardless of surface. These conditions require proper management rather than just switching from outdoor to treadmill running.

## The case for incline walking instead

For anyone with knee sensitivity, incline walking on a treadmill is one of the best alternatives available. At a steep incline — 10–15% — you get cardiovascular demand and calorie burn comparable to jogging, with ground reaction forces of roughly 1.2–1.5 times bodyweight rather than the 2–3 times bodyweight of running.

The mechanics are fundamentally different. Walking at any incline involves a smooth, controlled weight transfer with each step rather than the landing impact of a running footstrike. Tendons, ligaments, and cartilage experience a fraction of the stress they would under equivalent running intensity.

For the calories burned comparison at different inclines and speeds, the [treadmill calorie calculator](/calculators/treadmill-calorie-calculator) gives an accurate figure using the ACSM equations. The [12-3-30 workout](/articles/12-3-30-workout-does-it-work) — 12% incline, 3mph, 30 minutes — is a structured protocol that produces similar calorie burn to a moderate run with essentially zero knee impact.

This is exactly why many runners with persistent knee issues find that switching to incline walking solves the problem entirely. The cardiovascular work continues. The knee stress stops.

## How to run on a treadmill without hurting your knees

**Increase volume gradually.** Follow the 10% rule — never increase your total weekly running time or distance by more than 10% from one week to the next. Most knee injuries in runners are overuse injuries that develop over weeks, not acute injuries from a single session.

**Shorten your stride.** A shorter, higher-cadence stride reduces the braking force at each footstrike. On a treadmill, try increasing your cadence by 5–10% while maintaining the same speed — you will take slightly more steps per minute with a slightly shorter landing stride. Most people find this feels awkward initially and more natural within a few sessions.

**Don't hold the handrails.** Holding the handrails changes your gait mechanics and reduces calorie burn. Walk or run without holding on — if you cannot, reduce the speed or incline until you can.

**Strengthen your posterior chain.** Weak glutes are implicated in a large proportion of running knee injuries. [Romanian deadlifts](/articles/best-leg-exercises), hip thrusts, and single-leg exercises like Bulgarian split squats build the hip and glute strength that keeps the knee tracking correctly during running. Resistance training 2–3 times per week alongside treadmill cardio reduces injury risk substantially.

**Warm up before running.** Starting a run cold increases injury risk. Five minutes at an easy walking pace — or on a low incline — before increasing speed and intensity allows the joints and connective tissue to warm up properly.

**Listen to knee discomfort early.** Pain that appears during a run and does not resolve within 24–48 hours is a signal worth acting on, not pushing through. Continuing to run on a symptomatic knee typically extends the total recovery time significantly. Taking a few days off when discomfort first appears is usually faster to resolution than running through it.

---

## When to see a doctor or physio

Knee pain that is sharp, persistent, swollen, or that locks the knee is worth getting assessed. Runner's knee and IT band syndrome are manageable with proper rehabilitation but benefit from early diagnosis and a structured programme rather than guessing.

If you have existing knee problems and want to continue treadmill cardio, switching to incline walking while you address the underlying issue is a practical interim approach — it maintains cardiovascular fitness and calorie burn without loading the symptomatic structures.

---

## The bottom line

The treadmill is not inherently bad for your knees. Running on a cushioned treadmill belt is actually modestly less stressful on the joints than running on concrete. Knee pain from treadmill use is almost always caused by running too much too soon, poor mechanics, or weakness in the muscles that support the knee — not the treadmill itself.

If knee discomfort is a limiting factor, incline walking is the best alternative — comparable calorie burn, comparable cardiovascular work, and a fraction of the joint impact. For most people with knee sensitivity, it removes the problem entirely while keeping the cardio going.
