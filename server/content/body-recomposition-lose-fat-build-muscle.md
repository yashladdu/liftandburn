## The fitness world's biggest myth — and the truth behind it

For decades, the conventional wisdom was clear: you cannot lose fat and build muscle at the same time. You must bulk (eat a surplus to build muscle, accepting fat gain) or cut (eat a deficit to lose fat, accepting muscle loss). The idea of doing both simultaneously was dismissed as impossible.

The research says otherwise.

Body recomposition — simultaneously losing fat and building muscle — is real. It is not easy, it is not fast, and it works better for some people than others. But for the right person with the right approach, it is absolutely achievable.

This guide explains exactly how.

---

## What body recomposition actually is

Body recomposition means changing your body composition — the ratio of muscle to fat — without necessarily changing your body weight significantly.

You might end the process at the same weight you started, but look dramatically different. Your clothes fit differently. You are stronger. Your body is visibly more muscular and leaner.

This is why the scale is a poor measure of recomposition progress. Someone successfully recomping might see their weight barely move for months while their body is transforming.

---

## Who can achieve body recomposition

Body recomposition is most effective for specific groups. The more of these apply to you, the more likely you are to achieve significant simultaneous fat loss and muscle gain.

**Beginners to strength training.** People new to lifting have the highest potential for recomposition. Your body responds powerfully to the novel stimulus of resistance training, building muscle even in a slight calorie deficit.

**People returning after a break.** Muscle memory is real. When previously trained muscle is lost (through injury, illness, or time off), it rebuilds faster than it was first built — even in a deficit.

**People with higher body fat percentages.** The more body fat you have, the more your body can draw on fat stores to fuel muscle building. People with 20%+ body fat have significantly more recomposition potential than lean, trained athletes.

**Intermediate lifters changing their training.** Someone who has lifted but never incorporated cardio — or vice versa — may experience recomposition effects when they add the new stimulus.

**Advanced athletes:** Recomposition is much harder for people who are already lean and experienced. For them, traditional bulking and cutting cycles are more efficient.

## The nutritional approach to recomposition

The key nutritional challenge of recomposition is that muscle building typically requires a calorie surplus while fat loss requires a deficit. The solution is to operate at or very close to maintenance calories while maximising protein intake.

### Calorie target

Eat at **maintenance calories** or a very small deficit (100–200 calories below maintenance).

This is not an aggressive cut. The goal is to create a metabolic environment that preserves and builds muscle while allowing fat to be used as fuel during training. Eating too far below maintenance kills muscle-building potential.

To find your maintenance calories, use a TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure) calculator online, or track your food intake for two weeks and average the daily calories at which your weight stays stable.

### Protein intake

This is the single most important nutritional variable for recomposition.

**Target:** 1.6–2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of bodyweight per day.

For a 80kg person, this means 128–176g of protein daily. This is substantially more than the average person eats, and substantially more than general health guidelines recommend.

High protein intake:
- Provides the amino acids required to build new muscle tissue
- Has the highest satiety per calorie of all macronutrients (helps you feel full in a deficit)
- Has a high thermic effect (your body uses more energy to digest protein than carbs or fat)
- Protects against muscle breakdown during calorie restriction

Good protein sources: chicken breast, turkey, eggs, Greek yoghurt, cottage cheese, fish, lean beef, whey protein.

### Carbohydrates and fats

With protein prioritised, distribute remaining calories between carbohydrates and fats based on personal preference. Both support training performance:

- Carbohydrates fuel lifting sessions and replenish glycogen
- Fats support hormone production, including testosterone

Neither needs to be eliminated. Extreme low-carb or low-fat approaches make recomposition harder, not easier.

## The training approach to recomposition

### Strength training: 3–4 days per week

Heavy compound lifting is non-negotiable for recomposition. Isolation exercises build muscle but compound movements — squats, deadlifts, bench press, overhead press, rows — build the most muscle mass most efficiently.

Programme requirements:
- Progressive overload every session (more weight or more reps than last time)
- 3–5 sets per exercise, 6–12 rep range
- Focus on the major movement patterns: squat, hinge, push, pull

### Steady-state cardio: 2–3 days per week

Zone 2 cardio (60–70% max heart rate) creates the calorie deficit required for fat loss without competing significantly with muscle building. It also improves insulin sensitivity, which enhances the body's ability to use carbohydrates for muscle building rather than fat storage.

Avoid HIIT during recomposition — it adds substantial recovery demand and makes maintaining the calorie balance much harder.

Target 90–120 minutes of Zone 2 per week, spread across 2–3 sessions.

## How long does body recomposition take?

This is where honesty matters. Recomposition is slower than aggressive bulking or cutting.

Realistic expectations:

| Timeframe | What to expect |
|-----------|---------------|
| 4–8 weeks | Strength improving noticeably, slight visual changes |
| 3 months | Clear visual difference in muscle definition, measurable fat loss |
| 6 months | Significant body composition change, considerably stronger |
| 12 months | Transformation-level results for most beginners |

Progress looks slow on the scale. Measure it instead with:
- Progress photos (same lighting, same time of day) every 4 weeks
- Body measurements (waist, hips, arms, chest) monthly
- Strength numbers on key lifts
- How your clothes fit

## The most common recomposition mistakes

**Eating too little.** A large calorie deficit kills muscle-building potential. Recomposition requires eating enough to support training — around maintenance, not 500 calories below.

**Not enough protein.** Most people eating at maintenance do not hit 1.6g/kg of protein. This single variable accounts for more failed recomps than any other.

**No progressive overload.** If you are not getting stronger over time, you are not building muscle. Your training needs to demand more of your body each week.

**Expecting scale weight to change dramatically.** The scale will barely move during successful recomposition. This is normal and expected. Do not let it discourage you.

**Trying HIIT instead of Zone 2.** HIIT is harder to recover from and makes calorie management more difficult. Zone 2 is the tool for recomposition cardio.

## The bottom line

Body recomposition is not a shortcut. It is a slower, steadier process than aggressive bulk and cut cycles. But for beginners, returning athletes, and people with higher body fat, it produces real, visible results without the psychological difficulty of extreme cutting or the frustration of intentional fat gain during a bulk.

The formula is not complicated: eat at maintenance, prioritise protein, lift heavy with progressive overload, and add steady-state cardio. Do this consistently for 6–12 months and the results speak for themselves.
