## The protein myth the supplement industry built

Walk into any gym, open any bodybuilding forum, or read the label on a tub of whey protein and you will encounter the same advice: eat more protein. A lot more. Two grams per pound of bodyweight. Three grams. As much as you can stomach.

This advice is not based on science. It is based on marketing.

The supplement industry profits when you believe your protein intake is always insufficient. Protein powder sales depend on the idea that you are perpetually under-consuming. The reality — supported by decades of research — is considerably more modest, and considerably more liberating.

You need far less protein than you have probably been told. And the calories you are not spending on excess protein can go toward carbohydrates and fats that actually fuel your training and support your recovery.

---

## What protein actually does

Before the numbers, a quick reminder of why protein matters at all.

Muscle tissue is made of protein. When you train, you create small amounts of damage to muscle fibres. Your body repairs this damage — and adds a little extra — using amino acids derived from the protein you eat. This is muscle protein synthesis, and it is the mechanism behind all muscle growth.

For this process to work optimally, you need to consume enough protein to:

1. Cover your baseline protein needs for normal bodily function
2. Provide sufficient amino acids to support the repair and growth of muscle tissue beyond baseline

The question is: how much is enough? And crucially — how much is too much, where additional protein stops contributing to growth and simply gets used for energy or excreted?

---

## The evidence-based target: 0.7–0.8g per pound of bodyweight

The most comprehensive meta-analysis on protein and muscle growth, published in the British Journal of Sports Medicine and covering 49 studies and 1,800 participants, found that protein intake beyond **1.62g per kilogram of bodyweight per day** produced no additional muscle growth.

Converting to the numbers most people use:

| Unit | Minimum | Ideal | Beyond this = diminishing returns |
|------|---------|-------|----------------------------------|
| Per kg of bodyweight | 1.6g | 1.8g | 2.2g+ |
| Per lb of bodyweight | 0.7g | 0.8g | 1.0g+ |

**0.7g per pound is the minimum** — the floor below which muscle growth starts to be limited by protein availability rather than training stimulus.

**0.8g per pound is the ideal** — the sweet spot that covers your needs comfortably with a small buffer, without wasting calories on protein your body cannot use for muscle building.

## What this looks like in practice

Here is the daily protein target for different bodyweights at both the minimum and ideal intake:

| Body weight | 0.7g/lb (minimum) | 0.8g/lb (ideal) |
|-------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| 60 kg (132 lbs) | 92g | 106g |
| 70 kg (154 lbs) | 108g | 123g |
| 80 kg (176 lbs) | 123g | 141g |
| 90 kg (198 lbs) | 139g | 158g |
| 100 kg (220 lbs) | 154g | 176g |

For a typical 80kg person, the ideal target is approximately **140g of protein per day**. Not 200g. Not 250g. 140g — which is achievable through normal food without forcing down multiple protein shakes or choking on plain chicken breast at every meal.

---

## You do not need to eat enormous amounts of protein

This is the point most bodybuilding content refuses to make clearly.

The difference between 140g and 220g of protein per day for an 80kg person is 80g of additional protein — roughly 320 extra calories. Research consistently shows that those 320 calories contribute nothing additional to muscle growth. They are either burned for energy or, in excess, stored as fat.

More importantly, those 320 calories could instead go toward:

- **Carbohydrates** — your muscles' preferred fuel source during lifting, which directly improves training performance, recovery, and glycogen replenishment
- **Fats** — essential for hormone production including testosterone, which is a primary driver of muscle growth

There is a real cost to over-eating protein beyond your needs. It crowds out carbohydrates and fats that serve important functions in supporting the training that actually builds the muscle. A lifter eating 250g of protein on a 2,500 calorie diet has left only 1,250 calories for carbs and fat combined — not enough to fuel serious training.

## How protein works in your body

Your body processes protein differently depending on how much you consume in a given period.

At moderate intake levels, amino acids from digested protein are directed primarily toward muscle protein synthesis and other protein-based bodily functions. At very high intake levels, once these needs are met, the remaining amino acids are deaminated — the nitrogen is stripped and excreted via urine, and the carbon skeleton is used as energy, essentially the same as carbohydrate.

In other words: protein above your needs does not disappear, but it does not build extra muscle either. It simply becomes an expensive and less efficient source of calories compared to carbohydrates or fats.

This is why the research caps meaningful benefit at around 1.6–2.0g per kilogram. Your body has a ceiling for how much protein it can direct toward muscle building at any given time. Beyond that ceiling, the process is saturated.

## Protein timing matters, but not as much as total intake

You may have heard that you need to consume protein within 30 minutes of training — the so-called anabolic window. This has been significantly overstated.

The research on protein timing shows:

- **Total daily protein** is the primary driver of muscle growth — getting your daily target is far more important than when you eat it
- **Per-meal protein** has a practical ceiling of around 30–40g for maximising muscle protein synthesis from a single meal — eating 80g of protein in one sitting does not double the effect of eating 40g
- **Spreading protein across 3–5 meals** throughout the day is more effective than consuming the same total in one or two large meals
- **Post-workout protein** is beneficial but the window is much larger than 30 minutes — research suggests 2 hours either side of training is sufficient

Practically: hitting your daily protein target matters most. Spreading it reasonably across meals is helpful. Stressing about the exact timing of a post-workout shake is not worth the anxiety.

## The best food sources of protein

You do not need to rely on protein supplements to hit your target. Whole food sources are preferable — they come with additional micronutrients, satiate more effectively, and are generally more enjoyable to eat.

**High-protein foods and their approximate protein content:**

| Food | Serving | Protein |
|------|---------|---------|
| Chicken breast | 150g | 45g |
| Eggs | 3 large | 18g |
| Greek yoghurt | 200g | 20g |
| Cottage cheese | 200g | 24g |
| Canned tuna | 1 can (130g) | 30g |
| Beef mince (lean) | 150g | 35g |
| Salmon fillet | 150g | 35g |
| Whey protein shake | 1 scoop (30g) | 22–25g |

An 80kg person targeting 140g of protein daily could hit their target with:

- Breakfast: 3 eggs + 200g Greek yoghurt = 38g
- Lunch: 130g tuna + salad = 30g
- Dinner: 150g chicken breast + vegetables = 45g
- Snack: 200g cottage cheese = 24g
- **Total: 137g** ✓

No protein shakes required. No forcing down bland food. Just normal meals built around protein-dense whole foods.

## What to do with the remaining calories

Once your protein target is set, the rest of your calorie budget should be split between carbohydrates and fats based on personal preference and training demands.

**Carbohydrates** are the priority for anyone training seriously. Muscle glycogen — stored carbohydrate — is the primary fuel for resistance training. Low glycogen means weaker sessions, slower recovery, and ultimately less growth stimulus. People who chronically under-eat carbohydrates in favour of excess protein often find their training performance suffers noticeably.

A reasonable starting point for active lifters:

- **Protein:** 0.8g per pound of bodyweight
- **Fat:** 0.35–0.45g per pound of bodyweight (essential for hormones)
- **Carbohydrates:** remaining calories

For an 80kg person at 2,600 calories per day:
- Protein: 140g = 560 calories
- Fat: 70g = 630 calories
- Carbohydrates: 1,410 calories ÷ 4 = **352g of carbohydrates**

This is a performance-oriented distribution. It fuels training well, supports recovery, and provides adequate protein for muscle growth — without over-allocating to protein at the expense of the other macronutrients.

---

## The bottom line

You need 0.7–0.8g of protein per pound of bodyweight per day to maximise muscle growth. For most people this is between 120–180g daily — significantly less than the 200–300g figures commonly promoted in bodybuilding circles.

Beyond this threshold, additional protein does not build more muscle. It burns for energy or gets excreted, at the cost of the carbohydrates and fats that fuel your training and support your hormones.

Hit your protein target consistently. Build your meals around real food. Use the remaining calories to fuel your training with carbohydrates and support your hormones with fats.

That is the entire nutrition strategy for building muscle. Everything else is detail.
