## What the back actually needs

The back is a collection of muscles with two distinct movement patterns: **vertical pulls** (pulldowns, pull-ups) for lat width, and **horizontal pulls** (rows) for mid-back thickness. Cover both in your training and you've trained the back properly. A third category — hip-hinge movements like deadlifts — adds overall back strength and posterior chain development.

Train all back movements with **2 working sets, 1 RIR**. Progressive overload over weeks and months is what actually builds back thickness — no amount of volume replaces adding weight to the bar.

---

## Best Back Exercises

### 1. Lat Pulldown / Pull-Up

The primary vertical pull. If you can do pull-ups, do them — they're one of the best upper body exercises that exists. If you can't yet, the lat pulldown is equally effective for building muscle and trains exactly the same pattern.

Switch your grip periodically — wide, narrow, neutral (palms facing each other) — to hit different parts of the lats. A neutral or slightly supinated grip tends to allow the most range of motion and the strongest contraction for most people. Focus on driving the elbows down and back rather than pulling with the hands.

### 2. T-Bar Row (Chest Supported)

An outstanding mid-back and lat builder. The chest-supported variation is key: when your chest is braced against a pad, you can't generate momentum with your lower back and hips, which means every rep is the back doing the actual work. That's exactly what you want for hypertrophy.

Load it heavy relative to unsupported rows and really focus on squeezing the shoulder blades together at the top.

### 3. Chest Supported Row (Machine or Dumbbell)

Any chest-supported row variation follows the same principle — brace, eliminate momentum, make the back muscles do everything. Whether it's a machine, a dumbbell row with your chest on an incline bench, or a cable variant, the support removes the temptation to cheat the weight up.

Far more effective for muscle growth than bent-over barbell rows, where ego and lower back fatigue typically take over before the back muscles are close to failure.

### 4. Seated Cable Row

Run it one arm at a time for a greater range of motion and a stronger unilateral contraction, or two arms for heavier loading. The one-arm version lets you really reach forward at the start for a full lat stretch and drive the elbow back hard at the finish. Both versions work well — alternate between them.

A close-grip or neutral-grip handle typically produces the strongest contraction for the mid-back.

### 5. Single-Arm Dumbbell Row

A classic for a reason. The free-hand support on the bench lets you focus entirely on the working side. Use a full range — let the shoulder drop and the weight stretch the lat at the bottom, then drive the elbow up and back as high as it'll go. Don't rush the top contraction.

### 6. Deadlift (Any Variation)

Conventional, sumo, trap bar, Romanian — pick the one that suits your body and train it. Any deadlift variation builds serious posterior chain strength and overall back thickness that rows and pulldowns alone can't replicate. The trap bar deadlift is the most accessible for most people; the conventional barbell deadlift is the most transferable to powerlifting.

Don't overthink which variation is "best." The one you can load progressively and execute well is the right one.

---

## How to train back

A complete back session: one vertical pull and one horizontal row. Two sets each, 1 RIR. If you want more, add a deadlift variation on a separate day.

A strong pairing: **Lat Pulldown + Chest Supported Row.** Or **Pull-Ups + Seated Cable Row.** Train hard, progress the weights, repeat.
