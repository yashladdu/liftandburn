## The honest truth about abs first

Ab exercises build the abdominal muscles. Visible abs require low enough body fat. Those are two separate things — no amount of crunches burns the fat covering the abs.

Everyone has abdominal muscles. What determines whether you can see them is body fat percentage, which is dictated by diet and overall calorie balance. Building the abs with good exercises makes them thicker and more visible once the fat is low enough, but the exercises alone don't produce visible abs regardless of how many you do.

That said, a strong core improves every other lift, reduces lower back injury risk, and is worth training for its own sake. Here's how to do it properly.

**2 working sets, 1 RIR** applies here too. The abs respond to progressive overload like any other muscle group — add resistance over time rather than just adding reps indefinitely.

## Best Ab Exercises

### 1. Cable Crunch

The most underrated ab exercise and arguably the best for building actual abdominal muscle. Kneeling at a cable machine with a rope attachment, you can load the crunch progressively by adding weight — which is something floor crunches and sit-ups can never do. Progressively heavier cable crunches over months builds far more ab muscle than bodyweight movements alone.

Round your spine as you crunch down — focus on bringing your ribs toward your hips rather than just moving your head toward the floor.

### 2. Hanging Leg Raise

One of the best movements for the lower abs and the hip flexors. Hang from a pull-up bar and raise your legs, either straight or bent at the knee for an easier variation. The key is to tuck the pelvis at the top — avoid just swinging the legs up without any actual spinal flexion, which makes it a hip flexor exercise rather than an ab exercise.

Progress from bent-knee raises → straight-leg raises → toes-to-bar as you get stronger.

### 3. Ab Wheel Rollout

One of the hardest ab exercises available. From a kneeling position, roll the wheel forward until your body is extended, then contract the abs to pull back. The eccentric stretch on the rollout is where the difficulty lives. Start with partial range of motion and build up gradually — going too far too soon puts the lower back at risk if your abs aren't strong enough to control the movement.

Builds a strong, thick set of abs when trained progressively.

### 4. Plank and RKC Plank

Standard planks are fine for beginners — they teach core bracing. The **RKC plank** is a harder variation: squeeze everything simultaneously (glutes, quads, abs, fists) while holding the position. Much more demanding than a standard plank for the same duration.

Planks are primarily an anti-extension exercise — they train the core to resist being bent backward, which is highly functional for protecting the lower back during compound lifts.

### 5. Decline Sit-Up / Weighted Sit-Up

Adding a decline angle or holding a weight plate increases the demand on the abs through a fuller range of motion than a flat-floor sit-up. Keep the movement controlled — slow down on the way back rather than flopping back and bouncing up. A weight plate held at the chest makes these significantly harder without any special equipment.

### 6. Dead Bug

An excellent core stability exercise that's harder than it looks. Lying on your back, extend opposite arm and leg simultaneously while keeping your lower back pressed into the floor. The challenge is maintaining spinal position while the limbs move — which is exactly how the core functions in real life and during compound lifts. Great for lower back health.

### 7. Pallof Press

A cable or band anti-rotation exercise. Stand side-on to a cable, hold the handle at chest height, and press it straight out in front of you. The cable pulls you toward it; your core resists the rotation. Trains the obliques and deep stabilisers in a way that direct crunch-type movements miss entirely.

## How to train abs

Abs don't need to be trained every day — they recover like any other muscle. Two or three sessions per week is plenty.

A complete session: one **loaded flexion** movement (cable crunch or weighted sit-up) and one **anti-extension or stability** movement (plank, ab wheel, or dead bug). Two sets each, progressive resistance where possible.

For visible abs: train them consistently, but recognise that the diet side of the equation — specifically a calorie deficit and adequate protein to preserve muscle — is what actually gets them visible.
