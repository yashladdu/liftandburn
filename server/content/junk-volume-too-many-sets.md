## The uncomfortable truth about your training

You have been doing 20 sets for chest. You have been in the gym five days a week for two years. You are working hard — or at least you think you are.

And yet the progress has slowed to almost nothing.

The answer is probably not that you need more sets. It is almost certainly the opposite.

Most gym-goers are drowning in junk volume — sets that are hard enough to require recovery but not intense enough to provide a meaningful growth stimulus. It is the worst possible combination: high cost, low return.

---

## What junk volume actually is

Junk volume is not a reference to any specific number of sets. It is a description of sets performed at an intensity level too low to maximally stimulate muscle growth.

A set is junk volume when:

- You stop it because you hit a rep target, not because you approached failure
- You could comfortably do 4-5 more reps but rack the weight anyway
- Your heart rate barely elevated
- You spent more time resting between sets than performing them
- You could have a full conversation while doing it

The term comes from the concept of junk food — technically it counts as eating, technically it provides some calories, but the nutritional value relative to the cost is poor. Junk volume technically counts as training, and it causes some fatigue, but the growth stimulus relative to the recovery cost is poor.

---

## Why junk volume proliferated

The high volume approach to bodybuilding dominated the sport through the 1970s and 1980s largely because of who was promoting it. Arnold Schwarzenegger trained with enormous volume and won seven Mr Olympia titles. His book, The Education of the Bodybuilder, became the training bible for a generation.

What most people copying Arnold's approach failed to account for:

**Genetics.** Schwarzenegger had exceptional muscle-building genetics that would have responded to almost any training stimulus.

**Pharmacology.** Anabolic steroids dramatically increase recovery capacity, meaning the volume that would overtrain a natural lifter is well within recovery capacity for an enhanced one.

**Selection bias.** The champions who trained with high volume and succeeded became famous. The thousands who trained with identical volume and failed remained unknown.

The result was a cargo cult — people replicating the external behaviour (high volume) without understanding whether it was the cause of the results or simply correlated with them.

---

## The recovery cost of junk volume

Every set you perform costs recovery resources. Your body has a finite capacity to repair and rebuild muscle tissue between sessions — governed by sleep quality, nutrition, stress levels, and genetics.

When you perform 20 sets for chest, your body must recover from all 20 sets before meaningful growth can occur. If those 20 sets include 14 junk sets that provided minimal stimulus, you have tripled your recovery cost for no additional growth benefit.

This is why so many high-volume trainees plateau and stay plateaued. They are training enough to prevent recovery but not intensely enough to maximise stimulus. They are stuck in a purgatory of perpetual moderate effort.

Mike Mentzer described this as "digging a hole you cannot climb out of." Each junk set makes the hole slightly deeper. Eventually the recovery deficit is so large that growth stops entirely — and the typical response, adding more sets, makes it worse.

## How to identify junk volume in your own training

Ask yourself these questions honestly about your last training session:

**On your last set of bench press, could you have done 3 more reps?** If yes, that set was probably junk volume. You were working, but you were not working hard enough to maximally recruit the high-threshold muscle fibres that grow.

**Did your performance decline significantly from set 3 to set 8?** If you are doing 8 sets of bench and your reps are dropping from 10 to 6 to 4 to 3, sets 5-8 are likely junk. You are fatigued, not stimulated.

**Are your later sets worse than your first?** In a properly structured HIT approach, your one working set is your best set — freshest nervous system, most available motor units, maximum effort. In high volume training, your best effort is often your first set when you are fresh, and everything after is a diminishing return.

**Have you made meaningful strength progress in the last 3 months?** If your bench press, squat, and deadlift numbers have not moved in 3 months of consistent training, your programme is not working. More of the same is not the answer.

## The research on effective sets

The concept of effective sets — sets that are close enough to failure to provide meaningful stimulus — has gained traction in exercise science.

Research by Milo Wolf and others suggests that only sets performed within approximately 5 reps of failure provide substantial hypertrophic stimulus. Sets performed with 6 or more reps in reserve contribute little to growth.

If you are doing 20 sets per muscle group but most of them are performed with 6-10 reps in reserve, you may be getting the equivalent growth stimulus of 4-6 truly effective sets — but with the recovery cost of 20.

This reframes the volume debate entirely. The question is not how many sets you do. It is how many effective sets — sets close to failure — you do and can recover from.

---

## What to do instead

### Step 1: Cut your volume by 50-60%

This is not a permanent change — it is a diagnostic. If you are currently doing 20 sets for chest, drop to 6-8. If you are training 6 days a week, drop to 3-4.

For most people who have been doing high volume for months or years, this reduction will initially feel too easy. That feeling is a symptom of how accustomed you have become to going through the motions.

### Step 2: Increase the intensity of every remaining set

Every set you keep needs to be taken closer to failure than you are currently going. If your sets currently stop at RPE 7 (3 reps in reserve), push to RPE 9 (1 rep in reserve) or RPE 10 (failure).

This will feel very different. One truly maximal set is exhausting in a way that three moderate sets are not.

### Step 3: Track performance obsessively

With lower volume and higher intensity, you should start adding weight or reps to your lifts more consistently. If you are not progressing on your main lifts over 4-8 weeks, intensity is still not high enough.

### Step 4: Take recovery seriously

Lower volume only improves your results if you use the recovered capacity for actual recovery — sleep, nutrition, stress management. Replacing gym time with other physical stressors defeats the purpose.

## A sample week before and after eliminating junk volume

**Before (typical high volume chest session):**
- Flat barbell bench: 4 × 10 (RPE 7)
- Incline dumbbell press: 4 × 12 (RPE 7)
- Cable flyes: 3 × 15 (RPE 6)
- Pec deck: 3 × 15 (RPE 6)
- Push-ups to finish: 2 × failure

Total: 16 sets. Intensity: moderate throughout. Genuine failure: zero sets.

**After (HIT-influenced chest session):**
- Flat barbell bench: 2 warm-ups + 1 × 8 to failure + 2 forced reps
- Incline dumbbell press: 1 × 10 to failure
- Cable flyes: 1 × 12 to failure

Total: 3 working sets. Intensity: maximum on every set. Genuine failure: every set.

The second session takes 25 minutes. It is significantly harder than the first session despite being shorter. And for most natural lifters it will produce superior results.

---

## The bottom line

Junk volume is the silent killer of training progress. It fills your programme with the appearance of hard work while providing insufficient stimulus for growth and excessive demand on recovery.

The path forward is counterintuitive but well-supported by both science and the results of the greatest natural and near-natural bodybuilders in history: do less, do it harder, and recover properly.

Every set should earn its place in your programme. If a set is not taken close enough to failure to provide genuine stimulus, it is costing you recovery capacity that could be used for growth. Cut it.
