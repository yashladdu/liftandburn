## The debate that has divided bodybuilding for 50 years

On one side: Arnold Schwarzenegger, training twice a day, six days a week, 20+ sets per muscle group, winning seven Mr Olympia titles.

On the other: Dorian Yates, training four days a week, 45 minutes per session, one all-out set per exercise, winning six Mr Olympia titles.

Same sport. Same goal. Completely opposite approaches. Both producing arguably the greatest physiques in history.

So which one is right?

---

## Defining the terms

Before comparing them, we need to be precise about what each approach actually means.

**High Volume Training (HVT):**
- 15-25+ sets per muscle group per week
- Multiple exercises per muscle group
- Moderate intensity — typically 60-80% of 1RM
- Rarely trains to true failure
- Training frequency: 4-6 days per week
- Session duration: 60-90+ minutes

**High Intensity Training (HIT):**
- 2-6 sets per muscle group per week
- One primary exercise per muscle group
- Maximum intensity — always at or beyond failure
- Every working set taken to complete muscular failure
- Training frequency: 2-4 days per week
- Session duration: 20-45 minutes

The key distinction is not just volume or frequency — it is the relationship between effort and sets. HVT spreads effort across many sets. HIT concentrates maximum effort into as few sets as possible.

---

## The case for high intensity training

### 1. Motor unit recruitment

Your nervous system recruits muscle fibres in order of size — small, fatigue-resistant fibres first, then progressively larger fibres as the demand increases. The largest Type II fibres, which have the greatest growth potential, are only fully recruited when you are at or near the point of failure.

A set stopped at 8 reps when you could have done 12 never fully recruits your highest-threshold motor units. This means the most growth-prone muscle fibres are barely stimulated, regardless of how many such sets you do.

HIT solves this by demanding failure on every working set — guaranteeing full motor unit recruitment every time.

### 2. The stimulus is the signal, not the volume

Muscle growth is triggered by mechanical tension and metabolic stress. Once a sufficient stimulus has been delivered — one that crosses the threshold for triggering adaptation — additional sets do not add more growth signal. They add fatigue.

Mike Mentzer's analogy: if you need to tan your skin, you expose yourself to enough sunlight to trigger the response and then move out of the sun. Staying in the sun for three times as long does not make you three times as tan — it burns you.

### 3. Recovery capacity is finite

Every set you perform draws on your body's finite recovery resources — the hormones, nutrients, and cellular machinery required to repair and rebuild muscle tissue. High volume training can easily exceed this capacity, leaving the body in a state of chronic partial recovery.

This is not theoretical. It has a name: overtraining syndrome. And the symptoms — stalled strength, persistent fatigue, declining motivation — are extremely common among high-volume trainees who have pushed volume progressively higher chasing gains that never came.

### 4. Time efficiency

One set to failure per exercise. 20-45 minute sessions. Three to four days per week. For anyone who has spent years grinding through 90-minute sessions six days a week with plateauing results, the efficiency of HIT alone is worth serious consideration.

---

## The case for high volume training

### 1. More sets mean more total mechanical tension

The primary driver of muscle growth is mechanical tension — the force placed on muscle fibres over time. More sets means more total tension over the course of a week, and more total tension generally means more growth stimulus.

This is the volume-load argument, and it has decent research support. Studies consistently show a dose-response relationship between volume and hypertrophy up to a point — more sets produce more growth, up to approximately 10-20 sets per muscle per week for most people.

### 2. Skill acquisition and technical practice

Compound lifts are skills. More practice means better technique. High volume approaches provide more repetitions of the movement pattern, which improves form, builds connective tissue strength, and reduces injury risk over time.

### 3. Not everyone can train to true failure safely

Genuine muscular failure on a heavy squat or deadlift is dangerous without a spotter or specific equipment. High volume training at submaximal intensity is far safer for most lifters, especially those training alone.

### 4. Research tends to favour moderate-to-high volume

The majority of hypertrophy research using trained subjects suggests that 10-20 sets per muscle group per week outperforms very low volume approaches in most populations. Brad Schoenfeld's work in particular consistently supports moderate to high volume for maximal muscle growth.

| Athlete | Approach | Sets per muscle | Frequency | Result |
|---------|----------|----------------|-----------|--------|
| Arnold Schwarzenegger | High volume | 20-25 | 2x/day, 6 days | 7x Mr Olympia |
| Dorian Yates | Modified HIT | 3-5 | 4 days/week | 6x Mr Olympia |
| Mike Mentzer | Pure HIT | 1-2 | 3 days/week | Mr Universe (perfect score) |
| Ronnie Coleman | High volume | 20+ | 5-6 days | 8x Mr Olympia |
| Lee Haney | Moderate volume | 12-15 | 4-5 days | 8x Mr Olympia |

The uncomfortable truth: elite bodybuilders have succeeded with both approaches. This suggests that genetics, drugs, effort, and consistency matter more than the volume vs intensity debate for most people.

## The junk volume problem

Here is where the HIT argument is strongest and most overlooked.

Most high-volume trainees are not actually training at high intensity. They are training at moderate intensity for high volume. Their sets are taken to an RPE of 7 or 8, stopping well short of failure, accumulating volume without maximising stimulus per set.

This is junk volume — work that is hard enough to be tiring and to require recovery, but not intense enough to provide a maximal growth stimulus.

The result is the worst of both worlds: high fatigue, incomplete recovery, and a growth stimulus that is spread so thin across so many sets that no individual set is truly challenging the muscle.

Mike Mentzer's criticism was not of intelligent high-volume training — it was of the mindless volume accumulation that most people do when they follow high-volume templates without understanding the underlying principles.

---

## The practical answer

For most natural lifters, the truth lies somewhere between the extremes, but closer to the HIT end than most people currently train.

**What the evidence and real-world results suggest:**

- **2-3 hard sets per exercise** taken to or close to failure outperforms either 1 set (true HIT) or 6+ sets (typical high volume) for most people
- **6-12 sets per muscle group per week** is sufficient for most natural lifters — far less than the 15-25 sets commonly recommended
- **Intensity matters more than most people apply it** — if you are not regularly approaching failure, your volume is largely wasted
- **Recovery determines everything** — the right volume is the most you can fully recover from, not the most you can physically complete

---

## The bottom line

High intensity training wins on efficiency, recovery, and the quality of stimulus per set. High volume training wins on total mechanical tension and research support in general populations.

But the real insight from comparing these approaches is this: most people are doing high volume at low intensity, which is the worst possible combination. They would get better results doing less with more effort.

Whether you adopt full HIT principles or simply increase the intensity of your current programme, the direction of change for most lifters is the same — fewer sets, harder effort, more recovery.
